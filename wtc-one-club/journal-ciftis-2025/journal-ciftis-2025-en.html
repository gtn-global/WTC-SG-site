When Singapore Becomes a Starting Point, Not the Destination

——What I Saw at CIFTIS 2025

In September 2025, I was in Beijing for the China International Fair for Trade in Services (CIFTIS).

It was the first time the World Trade Centers Association (WTCA) had established its own pavilion at CIFTIS.

Over several days, World Trade Center representatives, business partners, and Chinese companies from different countries and regions came together. Some discussed international trade, others talked about taking Chinese businesses overseas, and many were focused on a much more practical question:

If a Chinese company truly decides to enter an overseas market, where should it begin?

Looking back, that is what stayed with me.

Not the size of the pavilion.

Not the number of events.

But a question that became increasingly specific:

How should Chinese companies actually enter Southeast Asia?

Not whether they should go.

Not whether Southeast Asia offers opportunities.

But—

How do you actually take the first step when entering an unfamiliar market?

From the Main CIFTIS Venue to the Reality Companies Face

On September 10, CIFTIS officially opened.

Crystal Edn, WTCA Executive Director-Member Services, attended the Global Trade in Services Summit at the opening of CIFTIS.

At a gathering like this, the discussion naturally revolves around broad themes: trade in services, global cooperation, and higher-level opening-up.

But at the concurrent 2025 China Enterprise International Compliance Forum, the conversation quickly became much more practical.

Zhao Zhongxiu, President of the University of International Business and Economics (UIBE), delivered a keynote speech on innovative development in trade in services and institutional opening-up. He spoke about creating new space for innovation in trade in services through institutional opening-up, alignment with high-standard international economic and trade rules, and the development of digital trade.

Zhu Xiaolan and Zhang Ke also participated in discussions on compliance for Chinese enterprises going global.

I came away with a very clear feeling:

Once a company goes overseas, the first door it often has to open is not the market. It is the rules.

Law, taxation, finance, data, intellectual property...

These issues rarely appear on the most visible pages of a company's brochure. But once a business enters an unfamiliar market, they quickly move from being "back-office issues" to very real questions that can determine whether the business can move forward.

That is why I increasingly believe that companies going global cannot only ask, "Where should we go?"

They also have to ask:

Once we get there, how do we operate?

A Closed-Door Meeting Made the Questions More Concrete

During CIFTIS, WTCA organized a closed-door exchange on Chinese enterprises going global.

Compared with a public forum, there was less grand language in the room.

People sat together and talked about more practical issues.

WTC Singapore was at the table as well, alongside representatives from the Saltillo World Trade Center, Tokyo World Trade Center, and Osaka World Trade Center.

Together, these four World Trade Centers connect three very different markets: Mexico, Japan, and Singapore.

On the other side of the table were Chinese companies considering their international expansion, including Beyondsoft (002649), MingYang Smart Energy (601615), and Beijing United Information Technology (603613)

Blake Zhang, Zhu Xiaolan, and I joined the conversation as well.

What I particularly appreciated about this format was its informality.

Once people no longer had to face a formal stage and could simply sit around a table and discuss specific issues, many things became much clearer.

What companies really want to know is rarely just that "overseas markets offer tremendous opportunities."

They want to know:

Who really understands the local industry?

Who can become a reliable partner?

How does capital move into the market?

How do you find customers?

How do you handle legal and compliance issues?

And if you enter a completely unfamiliar market and run into a problem, who do you turn to?

These questions may sound small.

But once a company actually starts building a business overseas, these seemingly small issues often determine whether it can move forward.

That also made me rethink the meaning of the World Trade Center network.

The Saltillo World Trade Center, Tokyo World Trade Center, Osaka World Trade Center, and Singapore World Trade Center are, of course, rooted in very different markets.

But what they can offer is more than simply a location.

If a company is in Beijing today and preparing to enter Japan tomorrow, what it may need is not another overview of the Japanese market, but someone who genuinely understands how business works there.

If it is preparing to enter Mexico, it needs local experience, resources, and trusted connections.

And if it is preparing to enter Southeast Asia, Singapore should not be the end of the journey.

The value of a network is not how many cities appear on the map. It is whether, when a company truly needs help, there is someone there it can reach.

Then Came the Southeast Asia Session

If the closed-door meeting made me rethink going global from the perspective of a global network, the Southeast Asia session at the WTCA pavilion gave me a much more concrete view of the challenges companies face when entering the region.

The theme of the roundtable was:

"Southeast Asia Session: Singapore, Your Gateway to Southeast Asia."

Zhu Xiaolan, Chair of the WTCA Asia Pacific Trade Services Member Advisory Council, opened the discussion with a point that stayed with me:

"Southeast Asia is no longer an option. It is a must-answer question for Chinese companies going global."

I remember that line.

Because today, the question for Chinese companies is no longer whether they should enter Southeast Asia.

More and more companies are already here.

The real question is:

Once they arrive, how do they actually enter the local market?

The discussion that followed was highly practical.

Ting Liu, China Business Development Director at Lijie Asia, spoke about compliance.

She reminded companies that legal systems differ significantly across Southeast Asian markets. Cutting due-diligence costs at the beginning can ultimately result in costs many times greater later.

She also pointed out that Singapore's advantages extend beyond finance and taxation. Its internationally recognized legal system can provide an important layer of institutional support for companies conducting cross-border business.

Jie Pu, President of the Beijing Branch of DBS Bank (China) Limited, spoke about finance.

She specifically warned companies not to assume that once money has been transferred overseas, it can simply move freely.

Markets such as Vietnam and Thailand have their own foreign-exchange rules. Companies should begin designing their financial structures early, rather than waiting until problems emerge.

Le Zhang, General Manager of Cross-Border E-commerce Marketing at Homestyler, spoke about localization.

He said something that I have remembered ever since:

"It is not about going out. It is about going in."

I think that is exactly right.

For Homestyler, localization is not simply translating a product into another language. It means rethinking the local consumer. The company has even established local design teams in Malaysia and Indonesia to rethink product experiences from the ground up.

Ke Chen, Executive Secretary-General of the Cross-Border E-commerce Committee of the Hangzhou E-commerce Association and Founder of Hangzhou Jiuzijue Culture Media, spoke about branding.

A brand that already has strong recognition in China may effectively become a blank sheet of paper in Southeast Asia.

Religion, festivals, family values, consumption habits, and aesthetic preferences can all influence whether a brand is ultimately accepted.

Listening to all of this, the idea of "Singapore as a gateway" became much more concrete to me.

Singapore's role is not to make companies stop in Singapore.

Quite the opposite.

It should help companies begin from a relatively familiar and highly international environment, gradually understand the region, and then move into Southeast Asia's more complex and diverse markets.

"Going Out" and "Going In" Are Two Different Things

I increasingly believe that what Chinese companies need to accomplish today is the transition from "going out" to "going in."

Selling a product overseas is not the same as internationalization.

Registering an overseas company is not the same as internationalization.

Opening an overseas office is not the same as truly entering a local market.

To really "go in" means understanding local customers.

Understanding local laws.

Understanding the local financial system.

Understanding the local culture.

Understanding local business relationships.

And sometimes even rethinking how your own product and brand should exist in that market.

That is why I sometimes feel that:

Going global is a lot like starting a business all over again in another country.

That is also why I saw such a clear connection between the closed-door meeting and the Southeast Asia session.

Whether we were talking about Chinese companies such as Beyondsoft (002649), Ming Yang Smart Energy Group Limited (601615), and Beijing United Information Technology (603613), or international network nodes such as the Saltillo World Trade Center, Tokyo World Trade Center, Osaka World Trade Center, and Singapore World Trade Center, the underlying question was ultimately the same:

How does a company find its place in an unfamiliar market?

This Is Also Why I Increasingly Understand WTC Singapore

When I attended CIFTIS, I was still observing, learning, and thinking through these questions.

Today, as Secretary General & COO of WTC Singapore, I have a more concrete understanding of them.

Singapore can be a gateway.

But a gateway only has meaning if there is a road beyond it.

So I do not see the role of WTC Singapore as simply bringing companies to Singapore.

What we hope to do is:

Help companies start from Singapore and connect with the broader Southeast Asian market and the global business network.

Connect capital with projects.

Connect technology with industry.

Connect companies with markets.

And, more importantly, connect a company with the people it genuinely needs to know in an unfamiliar market.

That was one of my deepest takeaways from the closed-door meeting.

When the Saltillo World Trade Center, Tokyo World Trade Center, Osaka World Trade Center, and Singapore World Trade Center sit around the same table, they do not simply represent four locations.

They represent an answer to a much more important question:

When a company moves into a different market, who can it turn to next?

Some Things Seemed Small at the Time. Later, I Realized They Mattered.

Looking back, many of the things that happened during CIFTIS 2025 were actually quite small.

A forum.

A roundtable.

A closed-door meeting.

An exchange of contact information.

A brief conversation after an event.

At the time, no one knew whether any particular conversation would eventually become a project.

But that is how international business relationships are built.

It might be a phone call.

It might be an introduction.

It might be reconnecting several months later.

Or it might be a company encountering a problem and suddenly remembering someone it met in Beijing months earlier.

I increasingly believe that internationalization is never a single act of "going out."

It is built through countless real connections.

And a good international network is not made up of points on a map.

It is made up of people who are willing to answer the phone, introduce a friend, and share what they know about their market.

After CIFTIS, I kept thinking about something Zhu Xiaolan, Chair of the WTCA Asia Pacific Trade Services Member Advisory Council, said at the end of the Southeast Asia session:

"Singapore is a gateway, but it is not the destination."

At the time, it sounded like a summary of that particular discussion.

Looking back now, I think it also captures something I have come to understand more deeply about WTC Singapore.

Singapore can be a gateway.

But what really matters is what happens after you cross it — whether you can find people you trust and continue the journey together.

Perhaps that is where an international business network truly creates value.

Not by telling a company:

"There is a market here."

But by telling it, when it is ready to take the next step:

"I know someone there."

And many of the things that matter most may begin with exactly those words.
